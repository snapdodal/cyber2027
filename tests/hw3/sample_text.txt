The village library stands at the end of a narrow street, between the bakery and the old post office. It was built more than a hundred years ago by a teacher who believed that every child should be able to borrow a book without paying for it. In the beginning there were only three shelves and a single wooden table, and the teacher herself wrote the name of each reader in a thick green notebook.

Today the building has two floors and a small garden where people sit and read when the weather is warm. The children come after school to finish their homework, and the older visitors arrive in the morning to read the newspapers and to talk quietly about the news. On the second floor there is a room with computers, and next to it a quiet corner where the oldest books are kept behind glass.

The librarian knows almost everyone in the village. She remembers which stories each reader enjoyed and she often leaves a note inside a book to suggest the next one. When someone returns a book late she never asks for money. Instead she asks the reader to tell her the best sentence in the whole book, and she writes these sentences on small cards that hang near the entrance.

Every year in the spring the library holds an evening of stories. The lights are turned down, the chairs are moved into a circle, and anyone who wishes may stand and tell a tale. Some of the stories are true and some are invented, and the listeners are never told which is which. The evening ends with tea and with sweet bread from the bakery next door, and people walk home slowly through the dark streets, still thinking about what they heard.

Visitors from the city are sometimes surprised that such a small place has so many readers. The answer is simple. The teacher who built the library understood that a book is not only a thing to be kept on a shelf. It is a meeting between the person who wrote it and the person who reads it, and a village that reads together has a great deal to talk about.
